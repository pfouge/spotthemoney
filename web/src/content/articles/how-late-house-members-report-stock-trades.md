Half of the House stock trades disclosed so far in 2026 reached the public within 25 days of the trade. About one in eight took longer than the law allows, and most of those took more than three months.

Those figures come from the periodic transaction reports that House members filed electronically and that this site published between January 1 and November 12, 2026: 396 reports from 100 members, holding 3,716 purchases and sales.

## How long does a member have to report a trade?

The STOCK Act of 2012 requires a member of Congress to report a purchase or sale of a security worth more than $1,000 within 30 days of being told about it, and in no case more than 45 days after the trade. The report covers trades by the member, a spouse and dependent children.

Because a report does not have to say when the member learned of a trade, the only deadline anyone outside can check is the outer one. This site counts the days from the trade date to the date the report was filed and marks a trade late when that number is above 45. The guide to [late congressional trade disclosures](/guides/late-congressional-trade-disclosures/) covers the rule and the penalty.

## How quickly were House trades reported in 2026?

| Days from trade to report | Trades | Share |
|---|---|---|
| 7 days or fewer | 281 | 7.6% |
| 8 to 45 days | 2,983 | 80.3% |
| 46 to 90 days | 104 | 2.8% |
| More than 90 days | 348 | 9.4% |
| All | 3,716 | 100% |

The middle of the range is where almost everything sits. Four trades in five were reported after the first week and before the 45-day limit, and the median was 25 days. Very few reports arrive quickly: 281 trades, 7.6%, were public within a week.

A reader who wants to follow these trades as they happen should keep that delay in mind. A report filed today most often describes a trade made three to four weeks ago.

## How many were late?

452 trades, 12.2% of the total, were reported more than 45 days after the trade date.

Lateness in these records is lopsided. Of the 452 late trades, 348 were more than 90 days old when they were reported, and only 104 were between 46 and 90 days old. A report that misses the deadline by a few days is the less common case. The more common case is a single report that lists many trades from months earlier. The oldest trade disclosed this year was 866 days old when its report was filed.

The late trades came from 28 members. The other 72 members who reported a trade this year had none past the 45-day mark.

## What do these numbers leave out?

Three limits matter when reading them.

First, they cover the House only. Senate reports are published on this site in batches and run through October 1, 2026, and corrected Senate reports restate old trades in a way that would distort a count of delays, so they are kept out of this article.

Second, they cover electronic reports. A small number of House members file on paper, and those scanned reports are held back until they have been transcribed and checked. They are not in the counts above.

Third, a count of days says nothing about cause. A report can be late because a broker's statement arrived late, because a trade in a managed account was noticed late, or for reasons the filing does not give. The filing shows two dates, and that is all this article measures.
