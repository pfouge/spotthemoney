Corporate insiders sell far more often than they buy, and the last 90 days were no exception. In the Form 4 filings on this site, open-market sales outnumbered open-market purchases by about 16 to 1, and seven in ten of the sales were made under a trading plan set up in advance.

The figures cover filings disclosed in the 90 days to November 13, 2026 for the companies this site tracks, about 975 of them. They count two kinds of transaction only: open-market purchases (code P on the form) and open-market sales (code S). Option exercises, share awards, gifts and shares withheld for tax are left out, because they do not involve a decision to buy or sell at the market price.

## How much buying and how much selling?

| | Purchases | Sales |
|---|---|---|
| Lines reported on Form 4 | 645 | 10,502 |
| Insiders | 238 | 1,626 |
| Companies | 143 | 563 |
| Lines marked as under a 10b5-1 plan | 63 | 7,308 |

A line is one row on the form. A single sale carried out through the day at several prices is often reported as many lines, which is one reason the sale count is so much larger. Counting people gives a steadier picture: about seven insiders sold for every one who bought.

Of the 636 companies with any open-market trade by an insider, 493 saw only sales, 73 saw only purchases and 70 saw both.

## Why is selling so much more common?

Most insiders receive a large part of their pay in stock. Selling some of it is how that pay becomes cash, pays a tax bill or spreads a concentrated holding. None of that needs a view about the share price. Buying is different: an insider who buys on the open market is paying with their own money for more of a stock they already hold a good deal of.

That is the usual argument for paying more attention to purchases than to sales, and the guide to [insider buying and insider selling](/guides/insider-buying-vs-insider-selling/) sets it out together with its limits. A filing records a transaction. It does not record a reason.

## How much of the selling was planned?

About 70% of it: 7,308 of the 10,502 sale lines carried the Rule 10b5-1 box. A 10b5-1 plan is a written instruction, adopted in advance, to sell set amounts on set dates or at set prices. Since 2023 the form has a box an insider must tick when a trade was made under one.

A sale under a plan was decided weeks or months before it happened, so its date carries less information than the date of an unplanned sale. That is why the trade map on this site leaves plan trades out by default. Purchases are seldom planned this way: 63 of the 645 purchase lines were marked. The guide to [Rule 10b5-1 trading plans](/guides/rule-10b5-1-trading-plans/) covers how the plans work and the cooling-off periods that apply.

## How did 2026 look month by month?

| Month disclosed | Purchase lines | Sale lines | Insiders buying | Insiders selling |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| January | 126 | 1,995 | 55 | 473 |
| February | 216 | 3,452 | 113 | 1,066 |
| March | 229 | 3,819 | 130 | 975 |
| April | 97 | 2,766 | 82 | 501 |
| May | 349 | 4,298 | 200 | 882 |
| June | 242 | 5,457 | 133 | 790 |
| July | 111 | 3,358 | 67 | 536 |
| August | 310 | 4,991 | 160 | 1,051 |
| September | 198 | 3,716 | 86 | 641 |
| October | 196 | 2,456 | 75 | 504 |

The rhythm follows the earnings calendar. Most companies bar insiders from trading in the weeks before quarterly results, so trading bunches in the weeks after results come out. January, April, July and October, the months when most companies are waiting to report, had the fewest insiders trading, and the table shows it for buyers and sellers alike. May had the most insiders buying, 200, and January the fewest, 55.

## Where did several insiders buy at once?

Three or more insiders buying the same stock on the open market within 30 days is what this site calls a cluster buy. In the 30 days to November 13, filings on this site show that pattern at Matador Resources (nine insiders, November 5 to 10), Beacon Financial (seven insiders, October 26 to 30) and Amdocs (five insiders, November 9 and 10), among others. The filings give the dates, the share counts and the prices. They do not say what the buyers expected.

## What should a reader keep in mind?

These counts describe the companies this site tracks, which lean toward large companies and toward stocks that members of Congress have traded. They are not a count for the whole market. Funds and other large holders sometimes report the same trade on more than one form, and the counts above are lines as filed. And every figure here is a count of filings, with no share prices behind it: this article says nothing about how any of these stocks moved afterwards.

Every filing counted here is on the [Insiders page](/insiders/) with a link to the original on the SEC's EDGAR system, and the transaction codes are explained in the guide to [Form 4 transaction codes](/guides/form-4-transaction-codes/).
